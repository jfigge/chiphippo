The Multimeter

The multimeter is a bench digital meter you put beside the board and touch to the circuit with two probes. It is modelled on a real meter, the Brymen BM235: the same dial, the same jacks, the same input resistance and fuses — so it reads what a real one would, and goes wrong the way a real one does when it is used wrongly. It reads under Spice Lite only (Settings ▸ Spice Lite); with the logic engine the toolbar does not offer it and a meter already on the desk stays dark.

The Add Multimeter button on the toolbar (beside the Analyzer) takes a meter out onto the desk, at the right of the view, beside any already there. You can have up to three; the button greys out at three. To put one away, right-click it and choose Put Away. Taking one out and putting one away are ordinary edits (⌘Z undoes them), and both work while the circuit runs. It is drawn at its real size — about as tall as a full breadboard is wide.

The meter is not part of the circuit: it lies ON the desk, over the boards and everything on them. Drag it by its body anywhere — onto a board too, and while the circuit runs. It is never selected or highlighted, and whatever is under it cannot be reached until it is moved out of the way; only its own probes and leads lie on top of it.

The probes

Each probe trails a lead back to its jack: black from COM, red from whichever jack its plug is in. Drag a probe by its body onto any hole — an empty one, or one a chip's pin or a wire's end is already in (a probe touches the lead there; it takes nothing). A probe snaps to the nearest hole in reach, as a wire end does; dropped clear of every hole it lies on the desk where you let it go, touching nothing. A probe can also rest on a power supply's terminal.

A hole under a wide (600-mil) chip's body — the rows between its two rows of pins — takes no probe: no tip reaches it there. And a probe is pushed out of its hole by what is pressed into it: seat a wide chip over it, or push a part's lead or a wire's end into the hole it is in, and the probe lies on the desk where its tip was. (Put a probe on a hole a lead is already in and it stays: it touches the lead.)

Probes, the dial and the buttons all work while the circuit runs — a meter is used on a live circuit. Hold Option/Alt over a probe to reach the lead in the hole under it. Moving a probe, turning the dial and moving the plug are ordinary edits: ⌘Z undoes them.

The dial

Click a position's legend to turn the dial there. SELECT steps through what that position offers:

Position SELECT Measures
AutoV LoZ — DC or AC volts, whichever is larger, through a low 2.1 kΩ input
OFF — nothing
V DC, AC volts: 6 V, 60 V, 600 V and 1000 V ranges
Ω ohms, continuity resistance, 600 Ω to 60 MΩ; continuity beeps below 161 Ω
mV DC, AC millivolts: 60 mV and 600 mV
→| diode, capacitance diode test: a junction's forward drop, up to 3 V; capacitance, 20 nF to 10 mF
mA A DC, AC current: 60 mA and 600 mA in the µA mA jack, 6 A and 10 A in the 10A jack
µA DC, AC current: 600 µA and 6000 µA, in the µA mA jack

The display ranges itself (AUTO). RANGE holds the range it is on; each press after that steps to the next; hold it for a second to go back to auto-ranging. OL means the reading is past the range — or, on ohms, an open circuit.

The buttons act when they are let go, and a button held for a second does something else, as on the real meter:

Button Press Hold a second
SELECT the position's next function the backlight on or off (it goes out by itself after 10 minutes)
RANGE hold the range, then step it — in Hz, the trigger range back to auto-ranging
REC start recording; then show MAX, MIN, AVG and the present reading in turn stop recording
HOLD freeze the display, or let it go Δ Relative Zero: show every reading less the one shown now; again to stop
Hz the frequency of what the position measures, or back to it —
  • REC keeps the largest, the smallest and the average of the readings the display has shown since it started, and beeps at each new MAX or MIN (with Sound on). The display still ranges itself.
  • Hz works on V, mV and the current positions. It counts the signal's rising crossings through a trigger band about its average, so a logic signal reads whatever level it sits at; on V it triggers on the range the display was on, and RANGE steps the trigger range (6 V is the most sensitive, about 0.1 V RMS). Below 2 Hz it reads 0. A fast oscillator the simulation draws slowed (one past 1 kHz) reads its TRUE rate.
  • REC, Relative Zero and Hz are not available on AutoV, and turning the dial ends them.

The display updates five times a second, and shows the AVERAGE over that time, as a real meter does: a 50 % PWM on DC volts reads half its swing, and an AC range reads the true RMS of the waveform. Once it has found a signal's frequency, DC is averaged over whole periods of it, so a pure sine reads 0.000 V DC.

It is not an ideal meter

Whatever the dial selects is a real element between the probes, and the circuit sees it:

  • Volts puts 10 MΩ across what it measures. A divider of two 1 MΩ resistors off 5 V reads 2.381 V, not 2.500 — exactly what a bench meter shows. A net nothing drives reads 0.000 V: the meter's own 10 MΩ ties it to the black probe. (A TTL input on that same floating net reads HIGH — the meter and the chip are asking different questions.)
  • AutoV loads it with 2.1 kΩ, to drain the "ghost" voltages a high impedance meter shows. On a 10 kΩ divider that pulls the middle down to under a volt, and the display shows ----.
  • Ohms, continuity, diode test and capacitance push their own small test current through what they measure.
  • Current ranges put a small resistance in series — 100 Ω on µA, 1.9 Ω on mA, 0.04 Ω on 10A — and the circuit loses the voltage across it: on µA in series with 5 V and 1 kΩ the meter reads 4.545 mA, not 5.000, and an LED behind it glows a little dimmer. A meter straight in a supply's lead is the same resistance in that lead: everything past it is fed through it, exactly as through a resistor of that value, and the rail past the meter runs short by the drop — on µA, a chip drawing a few milliamps runs a few tenths of a volt short — while the supply's own terminals, and anything wired straight to them, keep the full voltage. In the − lead the ground past the meter stands above the supply's by the drop instead, and the chips on it drive and read from that ground. A meter in each lead carries the one current, each dropping its own; one meter past another drops on top of it. A capacitor on the rail past the meter charges through it, so a big one is a current surge through the meter at Run. A chip the drop takes under its minimum supply but above its family's floor (74LS 4.5–4.75 V, CD4000 2.7–3 V) still works and switches, and is said to be running on its margin; below the floor it is underpowered — just as it would be fed through a resistor.

Measuring current

A meter measures current by being IN the circuit: break the circuit, plug the red lead into µA mA (or 10A for more than 600 mA), turn the dial to µA or mA A, and put the probes on the two sides of the break. The meter closes the gap and reads what flows through it. Put both probes on one connected net and it reads nothing — no current has any reason to go through it.

On a current range the meter is very nearly a wire. Put it across a supply and it shorts it: the µA mA input's 0.4 A fuse blows (F1), the display reads nothing, and the circuit it was in is open. The 10A input has an 11 A fuse (F2); a 1 A supply's current limit usually saves that one. A fuse heats with the current through it, so an AC current past its rating blows it as a DC one does — judged by its RMS: 0.41 A RMS blows F1, 0.399 A never does, and the warning quotes the RMS current, not a peak. A blown fuse is marked with the red (i) on the meter and a warning, and is replaced when the circuit stops — like a part's smoke, it is a mistake on the bench, not part of the design.

A red lead left in a current jack with the dial on anything else shows InEr and beeps — the real meter's warning. The 10A jack's shunt is wired to COM whatever the dial says, so "measuring volts" across a supply with the lead still in 10A shorts it.

When the circuit is stopped

Stopped, the bench is switched off: volts and current read nothing. Ohms, continuity, the diode test and capacitance still read — the meter's test source is the only thing driving the circuit, as on a real unpowered board. And it drives it in time, from the moment the probes touch (or the desk last changed where the probes reach — a supply put down, another meter or a part on a strip it does not reach leaves the reading alone): across a capacitor the ohms reading climbs as the test current charges it, until it reads open; an LED on the diode test glows faintly, lit by the meter's fraction of a milliamp.

On a live circuit these give false readings, as a real meter does — the circuit's own voltages are added to the meter's test voltage. Measure resistance and capacitance with the circuit stopped (running, capacitance reads OL).

Measuring capacitance

Turn the dial to →| and press SELECT: the display reads in nF, µF or mF. The meter charges what is between its probes one way and then the other, between two small voltages, and times the swing — so it shows ---- for as long as that takes: a moment for nanofarads, a few seconds for a thousand microfarads. A capacitor on its own reads its value exactly. A resistor or another part across it changes the timing and so the reading, as it does on a real meter — take one leg out to measure it. Past 10 mF it reads OL.

Look and size

Properties ▸ Look draws the meter as the orange VoltHippo — the app's own — or as the EEVBlog edition of the BM235. It is the same meter whichever look it wears. The look you pick is the one the next meter you take out wears too.

Properties ▸ Size shows the meter Full — its dial, buttons and jacks — or Compact: folded down to just its screen and the two jacks its leads are in. Clicking the title at the top of the meter (its name, or the Volt Hippo's logo) switches between the two as well. The screen stays where it is. Folded, it has no dial or buttons, so the leads stay in the jacks they were in — open it out to change them.

Where a meter lies and its size are not saved with the project: they are the session's, so moving or folding one is no change to save. Open the project again and each meter is laid out beside the circuit at full size, in its look, its dial, leads and probes as you left them.

Sound

The continuity beep, the InEr beep and REC's beeps are silent unless the meter's Properties ▸ Sound is on; the display's backlight flashes for continuity either way.

In 3D

The 3D view shows the meter with its probes on the holes they touch and their leads running round the meter to its jacks.

What it does not do

The BM235's temperature, VFD, non-contact voltage and auto power-off are not modelled. Its readings are exact: its accuracy figures are not added. AutoV's input stays at 2.1 kΩ at every voltage (the real one rises above 50 V).

The meter is test equipment: it is left out of the schematic, the BOM, the build steps and the AI builder, and the KiCad and Digital exports leave it out — saying so when it was closing a gap on a current range, which the exported circuit then has open.
